Where the numbers come from. Every field on this page is read straight from ESPN's public college-football and NFL scoreboard feeds — one poll serves the live table and the timeline both. Nothing is estimated or filled in, and the two projections at the bottom (endings and heat) are labelled as the models they are.
The clock does not tick between refreshes. It shows the last value the feed reported, so a displayed time is only as current as the age shown in the header. It is deliberately not interpolated — a locally counted-down clock would drift from the real one.
Channel colours describe one household. Orange is over the air in Chicago — CBS, NBC, FOX, ABC, CW, and nothing else. Green is the YouTube TV package — ESPN, ESPN2, ESPNU, FS1, NBCSN, CBSSN, ESPNews, SECN, ACCN, BTN, FS2, NFLN, TBS, TNT, USA, truTV. Purple, dashed is everything else, because a name on neither list is a stream. That is the whole rule: two lists, and no third category to argue about. Teal is kept for the one awkward case, a broadcast affiliate the feed flags as out-of-market, which is neither watchable here nor a stream. Both lists live at the top of the script as OTA and CARRIED — edit them and the chips, lanes, filter, weights and tallies all follow.
Linear TV only keeps the two lists and drops everything else. "No channel listed" means the feed carried nothing, not that the game is unavailable.
FCS is the only tag. FBS and the NFL are what you came for and need no label. FCS is the one you might want to skip, so it is the one that gets marked.
The drive. The football sits on the line of the team holding it, with the down and the yard line beside it, said the way you would say it out loud: "2nd & 2 (Opp 42)" is second down on the opponent's 42, "Own 12" is backed up in your own half. The feed writes the yard line as a team abbreviation, which is only "own" when that team is the one with the ball, so the abbreviation is matched against the possessing team; when it matches neither side the feed's own text is shown unchanged. It comes from the feed's possession text when that exists and from its yard line otherwise; when the feed carries neither, nothing is shown rather than a guess.
Score, then margin and total. The score column is only the score. The column beside it holds two things on the same two lines: the margin on the leading team's own line, and the total points on the other. So "+2" over "38" means the away side is up two in a game that has produced 38 points, and "38" over "+2" means the home side is. The margin never needs a sign worked out because its position is the sign. A tie has no leader, so the total sits centred on its own.
Line. The pregame spread and total, exactly as the feed carries them. It is what the game was supposed to be, not a live price, and it is blank when the feed has nothing.
Clock. Quarter above, time below, and nothing else. Time-left had its own column and its own big number, but above 15:00 all it ever said was "this game is not close to over", which the quarter already said. The small delta underneath is how many seconds since this game's score or clock last moved in the feed.
Channel chips. A streaming chip is itself the link when the payload carries a player URL. Cable and broadcast chips are never links, because there is nothing to open.
Leagues. FBS, FCS and NFL are one set of switches governing the whole page — the live table and the timeline below always show the same football day, and turning one off filters instantly from data already in hand rather than waiting for a fetch. FCS is off by default only because it is a hundred more games. On a day carrying more than one league, games outside FBS wear a small tag and FBS games wear none; a single-league day is untagged because there is nothing to tell apart.
Crunch time. A row is highlighted when the feed shows under 5:00 left in regulation and a margin of 16 or less. Overtime counts as 0:00 left, so OT games are always highlighted while they remain within 16.
Win%. The only number on this page that is not a fact from the feed. It is ESPN's own model estimate, carried on the last play of the same payload — so it costs no extra request and adds no latency — showing the favoured side and, when it moves between polls, the swing in percentage points. It is coloured and labelled differently from every other column for that reason. Games where the feed carries no probability show a dash rather than a computed stand-in.
Polling. One loop serves the whole page. On Auto it polls every 3 seconds whenever any live game is near the crunch window, and every 15 seconds otherwise; it also refreshes the instant the tab regains focus. Turbo is scoped to the league the close game is in — chasing one FBS finish does not drag a hundred FCS games along at the same rate, so those hold a ten-second floor, which is invisible at the idle rate and is most of the difference when FCS is switched on. The next calendar date is pulled at most once a minute and a feed that comes back empty drops to once every five minutes. The per-row Δ shows how long ago that game's score or clock last moved in the feed — a large Δ on a live game usually means a stoppage, not stale data; check the header pill for actual feed age.
Next crunch. The pill beside the feed age answers one question: when do you need to be back. It runs the same pace, stall and halftime model the endings table uses, but aimed at 5:00 of the fourth rather than at zero, so the two can never disagree about how fast a game is moving. Crunch needs a close clock and a close score at once; the clock is projectable and the margin four quarters from now is not, so only games already inside 16 points are counted. A game outside 16 is left out rather than assumed to tighten. When nothing live qualifies the pill falls back to the earliest game that has not kicked and goes dashed and grey, because that time says "if this one is close", not "this one will be". It turns solid orange the moment something is actually in crunch, and shows how many.
Alert on crunch time. The sound fires when a game crosses into crunch — under 5:00 of regulation with a margin of 16 or fewer — anywhere on the slate, not merely when a game turns up in whatever your filters happen to be showing. A game already in crunch when the page opened never fires it.
Mkt. The Mkt button is a convenience search built from a template you control — it is a search, not a resolved market link, so confirm you've landed on the right market before acting.
Schedule. The Schedule panel is addressed by college week, because that is how the college calendar is published. A week is really a span of dates, so the NFL games falling inside the same span come along carrying their own week number — the two calendars never line up and the header states both rather than pretending they do. It reads on demand only, never on a timer, and cannot slow the live table. Kickoff times are shown in your local zone; where ESPN flags a time as unconfirmed it reads TBD rather than a placeholder. The .ics export covers exactly the games on screen and omits TBD kickoffs entirely, since a wrong time in your calendar is worse than a missing one. Event end times are a flat 3.5-hour estimate and are labelled as such — the feed carries no scheduled end.
Watch links. The Watch / ESPN+ button and the timeline's ▶ open a player URL carried in that game's own payload — event links, competition links, and media objects on both geoBroadcasts and broadcasts are all scanned. The href decides, not the rel tag: ESPN marks the Gamecast link rel:["live","desktop","event"] once a game kicks off, so trusting the rel is exactly how a Gamecast link ends up on a Watch button. A /watch/player deep link outranks a bare ESPN watch URL, which outranks a partner player URL. When the payload carries no player URL there is no button at all — hover the dash to see why. A link to something that is not the game costs more than an empty cell does.
Streaming is the default, not the exception. FloSports, Midco, NEC Front Row, LHN, BYUtv, conference digital networks, a club's own syndication package and every service that hasn't been invented yet are streams, because the two channel lists are closed and nothing else gets in. Earlier versions tried to recognise streams by name and let new ones through as cable; there is no name-guessing left to get wrong.
The Watch index. When a game's payload carries no player URL, ESPN's public airings list is matched against it on both team names and a kickoff within three hours, and the matched airing's id becomes a player link. Hovering the channel says when a link came from the index rather than the payload. It is off in one click in Settings, it never overrides a payload link, and if the request is refused the status there says so and nothing else on the page changes.
Reloading. The last slate is kept locally and painted immediately, so a reload shows the real table at the real height instead of an empty box that fills in a moment later. Restored rows are never presented as fresh — the age pill always reads from the live feed. Rows hold a constant height and slide when they change position, so a row moving up the board is something you can see happen rather than something you have to notice after the fact.
